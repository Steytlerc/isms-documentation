    General Information¶

    The purpose of this document is to define the GDPR compliance policies and procedures for Community Fibre. It defines the scope under GDPR in terms of lawfulness, fairness, transparency, purpose limitation, data minimisation, accuracy, storage limitation, integrity, confidentiality, and accountability. It addresses compliance with legal obligations under ISO 27001 clause A.5.31, ensuring data protection practices are compliant and properly managed.

    Scope¶

    The scope of GDPR compliance for CFL is structured around seven key principles as outlined by GDPR experts:

    1. Lawfulness, fairness, and transparency¶

    Whenever you're processing personal data, you should have a good reason for doing so. GDPR terms this principle as lawfulness. Reasons for processing data can include:

    1. The user has given you consent to do so.

    2. You must do it to make good on a contract.

    3. It's necessary to fulfil a legal obligation.

    4. For protection of vital interests of a natural person.

    5. It's a public task done in public interest.

    6. You can prove you have legitimate interest, and it's not overridden by data subject's rights and interests.

    The concept of fairness laid out in the GDPR goes hand-in-hand with lawfulness. It means you shouldn't purposely withhold information about what or why you're collecting data. In other words, users wouldn't be surprised if they knew how you were using their data. Fairness means you won't mishandle or misuse the data you collect. We must record the rationale.

    Transparency is inherently linked to fairness: Being clear, open, and honest with data subjects about who you are, and why and how you're processing their personal data is the definition of transparency. By following it, you act fairly towards your data subjects.

    2. Purpose limitation¶

    The GDPR's second principle sets boundaries around using data only for specific activities. This purpose limitation means data is "collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes" only, as stated in the GDPR.

    Your purposes for processing data must be clearly established. And they must also be clearly communicated to individuals through a privacy notice. Finally, you must follow them closely, limiting the processing of data to only the purposes you've stated.

    If at any point, you want to use the data you've collected for a new purpose that's incompatible with your original purpose, you must ask specifically for consent again to do it --- unless you have a clear obligation or function set out in law.

    3. Data minimisation¶

    Only collect the smallest amount of data you'll need to complete your purposes. This is the GDPR principle of data minimisation. For example, if you want to gather subscribers for your email newsletter, you should only ask for information necessary to send out the newsletters. Avoid gathering personal data such as phone numbers or home addresses, which aren't directly related to your purpose.

    4. Accuracy¶

    It's up to you to ensure the accuracy of the data you collect and store. Set up checks and balances to correct, update, or erase incorrect or incomplete data that comes in. Also have regular audits on the calendar to double-check the cleanliness of stored data.

    5. Storage limitations¶

    According to the GDPR, you must justify the length of time you're keeping each piece of data you store. Data retention periods are a good thing to establish to meet this storage limitation policy. Create a standard period after which you'll anonymise any data you're not actively using.

    6. Integrity and confidentiality¶

    The GDPR requires you maintain the integrity and confidentiality of the data you collect, essentially keeping it secure from internal or external threats. This takes planning and proactive diligence. You must protect data from unauthorised or unlawful processing and accidental loss, destruction, or damage.

    7. Accountability¶

    The GDPR regulators know an organisation can say they're following all the rules without doing it. That's why they require a level of accountability: You must have appropriate measures and records in place as proof of your compliance with the data processing principles.  Supervisory authorities can ask for this evidence at any time. Documentation is key here. It creates an audit trail you --- and authorities --- can follow if you do need to prove responsibility.

    Governance Activities¶

    Management and organisational records management¶

    1. Records management organisation - Your business has defined and allocated records management responsibilities.

    2. Records management policy - Your business has approved and published an appropriate records management policy. This is subject to a regular review process.

    3. Records management risk - Your business has identified records management risks as part of a wider information risk management process.

    4. Records management training - Your business incorporates records management within a formal training programme. This comprises mandatory training with regular refresher material, and specialist training for those with specific records management functions.

    5. Monitoring and reporting - Your business carries out periodic checks on records security and there is monitoring of compliance with records management procedures.

    Records creation and maintenance¶

    1. Record creation - Your business has set minimum standards for the creation of paper or electronic records.

    2. Information you hold - Your business has identified where you use manual and electronic records keeping systems and actively maintains a centralised record of those systems.

    3. Information standards - Your business has processes in place to ensure that the personal data you collect is accurate, adequate, relevant and not excessive. You carry out regular reviews to remove any personal data or records that are out of date or no longer relevant.

    Tracking and offsite storage¶

    1. Tracking and offsite storage of paper records - Your business has tracking mechanisms to record the movement of manual records and ensure their security between office and storage areas and in instances where records are taken offsite.

    2. Offsite transfer of electronic records - Your business has appropriate measures in place to transfer of electronic records off-site and protect personal data from loss or theft.

    3. Secure storage of records - Your business stores paper and electronic records securely with appropriate environmental controls and higher levels of security around special categories of personal data.

    Access to records¶

    1. Access to records - Your business restricts access to records storage areas to prevent unauthorised access, damage, theft or loss. You should implement role-based access and check it regularly.

    2. Your business has a process to assign and manage user accounts to authorised individuals and to remove them when it is no longer appropriate.

    3. Business continuity - Your business has business continuity plans in place in the event of a disaster. This includes identifying records that are critical to the continued functioning or reconstitution of your business. You also routinely back up data that is stored electronically to help restore information if needed.

    4. Disposal of data - Your business has a retention and disposal schedule which details how long you will keep manual and electronic records.

    5. Your business has confidential waste disposal processes to ensure that records are destroyed to an appropriate standard.

    Data security controls¶

    • Use of the Island browser for critical applications such as HOBS will become mandatory. This will enable better protection of the data and a full audit trail to show downloads from the core platforms and prevents data from being transferred to unapproved (i.e. private) cloud services. (i.e. Gmail, WhatsApp)

    • All company laptop and mobile storage are encrypted via configuration policy.

    • Microsoft Cloud App Security policies are configured to prevent access to corporate services from unauthorised devices except via a browser (i.e. Edge, Chrome) but this only applies to Microsoft services. These will be replaced with Island enforcement.

    • Microsoft Intune application policies prevent access to corporate services from personal mobile devices without enrolment and management of apps (i.e. Outlook, Teams, OneDrive, etc.)

    • Microsoft 365 Purview (Data Security) data loss policies currently monitor outbound email for data containing names and addresses. Thresholds for data loss alerts are configured (more than 50 names or addresses) which routes outbound email to quarantine.

    • Writing to USB storage devices is blocked via companywide policy. An exceptions list is maintained for field and network engineers that require USB storage for configuration of network equipment.

    • Where data is stored in Domo, the owner of the source data shall be responsible for the downstream data retention policy.

    • Backup policy to be checked and backups audited to ensure they do not contravene the system policy.

    Data masking (new requirement -- added 25/09)¶

    To enhance data protection and comply with GDPR principles, particularly data minimisation, integrity, and confidentiality, Community Fibre implements data masking requirements for handling personal data in non-production environments, such as development, testing, and analytics.

    Data masking involves obscuring or anonymising sensitive personal information to prevent unauthorised access or exposure while maintaining data utility for legitimate purposes.

    Requirements for Data masking:

    1. Identification of Strictly Confidential data: All personal data elements (e.g., names, addresses, email addresses, phone numbers, financial details) must be identified and classified as sensitive prior to any masking activities. This classification shall align with GDPR definitions of personal data and special categories of data.

    2. Masking techniques: Appropriate masking methods must be applied based on data type and use case:

      a. Static Masking: Replace sensitive data with fixed fictional values (e.g., replace real names with \"John Doe\").

      b. Dynamic Masking: Mask data in real-time during access, ensuring original data remains unchanged in storage.

      c. Tokenisation: Substitute sensitive data with non-sensitive equivalents (tokens) that can be mapped back only by authorised systems.

      d. Anonymisation: Irreversibly alter data so it cannot be linked back to individuals, used when data is no longer needed in identifiable form.

    3. Scope of Application: Data masking is mandatory for:

      a. All personal data transferred to or used in non-production (i.e. Dev, UAT, SIT) environments.

      b. Third-party sharing where full data is not required (e.g., analytics or testing with vendors).

    4. Implementation and Controls:

      a. Automated tools (e.g., integrated into AWS, Asure, or other infrastructure) shall be used for masking to ensure consistency and reduce human error.

      b. Access to unmasked data is restricted to production environments and authorised personnel only, with role-based access controls (RBAC).
